# Thought Attribution Through Suggestion and Repetition

**Effects:** Increase suggestibility, Increase confusion, Undermine confidence.

**Possible objectives:** Increase the effectiveness of other techniques.

## The Proposed Technique

Imagine someone hears a spoken phrase and then is immediately accused of having thought or said it: "You were just thinking that."

The phrase is familiar because it was recently heard. The accusation offers a different explanation for that familiarity: that it originated in the person's own mind.

The proposed manipulation exploits a distinction between **recognising words** and **remembering where they came from**. A person might recognise the phrase without reliably remembering its source, or experience a fleeting feeling of having thought it without accepting the accusation as true.

## How It Might Work

- **Auditory exposure:** Hearing words can make them easier to recognise or bring to mind later.
- **Suggestion:** An accusation such as "You were thinking that" proposes a source for the familiar words.
- **Source-monitoring error:** People can sometimes confuse information they heard with information they imagined or generated themselves.
- **Sleep deprivation:** Poor sleep can impair attention and some kinds of memory, potentially making it harder to judge the origin of information accurately.
- **Repetition:** Repeated misleading suggestions can increase familiarity and, in some experimental settings, false recollections.

The further hypothesis is that repeated accusations made during sleep deprivation could reinforce an association that continues after normal sleep resumes. Someone might then **feel as though they thought a phrase while knowing they did not deliberately think it**.

A proposed objective would be to create confusion about the authorship of thoughts, or the impression that someone else knows what a person was thinking. The mechanism would depend on familiarity and attribution, not actual mind-reading.

## Cumulative Reinforcement (Proposed)

The hypothesis does not require every accusation to be accepted. Even when someone immediately rejects a false attribution, repeated exposure could make the **idea**, rather than the exact words, increasingly familiar or accessible.

If a later accusation coincides with a genuine source-memory error, that experience might strengthen the association between recognising the idea and feeling that it was self-generated. On this account, **rejected accusations could prime later encounters, while occasional misattributions could reinforce the apparent authorship**. The two effects might interact over time rather than operating as independent attempts.

Familiarity does not guarantee this feedback loop: repetition can also strengthen someone's recognition that an accusation is mistaken.

## What the Research Supports

**1. External-to-internal source errors — Supported.** Garrison et al. (2017) found that healthy volunteers sometimes misremembered perceived information as imagined. Their study concerns later judgments about the source of words, not an immediate experience of inner speech.

[Monitoring What Is Real — Garrison et al. (2017)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5312673/)

**2. Familiarity influencing source judgments — Supported.** Starns et al. (2008) showed that recognition of an item can influence judgments about its source, and that the framing of a source-memory question matters. This makes the proposed role of an accusation plausible, without demonstrating that a particular accusation would work.

[Cue-Framing Effects in Source Remembering — Starns et al. (2008)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2739603/)

**3. Sleep deprivation and false memories — Supported in some conditions.** Frenda et al. (2014) found increased false memories following misleading information when participants were sleep-deprived during initial event encoding. The effect was not significant when sleep deprivation occurred only after encoding.

[Sleep Deprivation and False Memories — Frenda et al. (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25031301/)

**4. Repetition and false recollection — Supported.** Zaragoza and Mitchell (1996) found that repeating misleading suggestions increased confident false recollections of events, with effects observed after delays of up to a week.

[Repeated Exposure to Suggestion and the Creation of False Memories — Zaragoza and Mitchell (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1996.tb00377.x)

**5. Feeling a memory without believing it — Supported for autobiographical events.** Mazzoni, Scoboria and Harvey (2010) studied *nonbelieved memories*: recollections that people still experience despite no longer believing the events occurred. This illustrates the separation between a memory-like feeling and conscious belief, although it was not a study of individual thoughts.

[Nonbelieved Memories — Mazzoni et al. (2010)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20689053/)

## What Remains Unproven

Whether repeated accusations—including those consciously rejected—can reinforce later false self-attribution, particularly after recovery from sleep deprivation. Testing a prolonged, coercive version of this hypothesis would present serious ethical difficulties.

## Claims presented as explanations

During a period of sleep deprivation, I was told that "super-advanced bugs" had been deployed in my house and could hear my subvocalisations. I was also told that I say everything I think out loud. I do not. No known listening bug can hear silent subvocalisations or unspoken thoughts from across a room.

## Response

**Recognising a phrase is not the same as having thought it.** A feeling of familiarity or remembering does not establish that the words originated in your mind, and an accusation is not evidence of what you thought.

There is no need to reconstruct every fleeting thought or debate someone's claim to know what was in your mind. If an incident needs recording, write down what was actually heard and said, separately from possible explanations.

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**
