# Priming, Ambiguous Accusations and Failed Attempts

**Effects:** Increase suggestibility, Increase confusion, Undermine confidence.

**Possible objectives:** Increase the effectiveness of other techniques.

## The proposed technique

The voice introducing a phrase wouldn't actually have to sound like yours for priming to work. Hearing a word or an idea can make it more accessible regardless of who says it.

Imagine that somebody speaks a phrase and, shortly afterwards, somebody else shouts, "He's just said he's a nonce."

The accusation is ambiguous. "He" could mean the person whose voice was heard earlier, the listener, or somebody else. The listener's own perception of events can fill in the missing meaning. If the subject was already on their mind, the statement might feel uncomfortably specific even though the speaker never actually identified anybody.

This is a proposed combination of ordinary psychological processes, not evidence that somebody can observe another person's thoughts.

## Several possible outcomes

- **The phrase is noticed and dismissed.** If the listener recognises the remark as negging, an insult or a provocation, they may simply write it off. Knowing that a remark can be manipulative does not require working out what it means.
- **The phrase primes an idea without the listener noticing its influence.** The later accusation could seem oddly familiar or produce a momentary feeling that somebody knew what the listener was thinking. The familiarity may be an effect of hearing the idea, rather than a sign that it originated in the listener's own mind.
- **The priming doesn't work.** The later accusation could still function as an insult, provocation or repetition of an unpleasant idea. This does not mean it necessarily changes anyone's beliefs.
- **Neither statement has a meaningful effect.** Nothing useful may happen at all. In a hypothetical low-cost repetition strategy, that doesn't prevent another attempt. But it is not a guaranteed-success technique: repetition can also become less effective, more conspicuous or easier to dismiss.

In other words, a failed priming attempt might have a separate effect, and a failed attempt need not stop future attempts. But repeated exposure does not guarantee reinforcement. It can also strengthen the listener's recognition that the statements are unreliable.

## Could a reaction tell somebody whether priming worked?

An observable reaction might show that a person noticed something, was startled or became uncomfortable. It would not reveal the specific thought they had, prove that semantic priming occurred, or reliably distinguish a primed association from an ordinary reaction to an insult.

The lack of a visible reaction is ambiguous too: the person might have processed the words, ignored them, recognised a familiar pattern, or not heard them clearly. **Not responding gives no reliable confirmation either way.**

## What the research supports

**Semantic priming need not use the listener's voice.** Experiments comparing same- and different-speaker speech find that the identity of the voice can influence some priming effects, but spoken-word meaning can still be processed across speakers.

Reference: *Processing Lexical and Speaker Information in Repetition and Semantic/Associative Priming* (2015). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28752195/

**Source monitoring can make familiarity misleading.** Remembering that a phrase is familiar is not the same as remembering whether it was heard or internally generated. Source judgments can sometimes be mistaken.

Reference: Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay (1993), *Source Monitoring*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346328/

**Pronouns are resolved using context.** A sentence containing "he" can be interpreted differently depending on the listener's expectations and attention. An interpretation supplied by the listener does not establish what the speaker meant.

Reference: *Does referential expectation guide both linguistic and social constraints on pronoun comprehension?* https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X22000882

**Repetition has competing effects.** Repeated statements may feel more familiar or credible, but repeated attempts at persuasion can also trigger scepticism and reduce credibility.

References: *The Effects of Repetition Frequency on the Illusory Truth Effect* (2021), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116821/ ; *Helpful or Harmful? How Frequent Repetition Affects Perceived Statement Credibility* (2013), https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12063

## Research gap

The individual effects are documented, but their combination into a reliably effective sequence of priming, ambiguous accusation, attribution and reinforcement has not been established by these studies. An observable reaction cannot validate a claim about somebody's private thoughts.

## Claims presented as explanations

During a period of sleep deprivation, I was told that "super-advanced bugs" had been deployed in my house and could hear my subvocalisations. I was also told that I say everything I think out loud. I do not. No known listening bug can hear silent subvocalisations or unspoken thoughts from across a room.

## Response

**Don't engage at all.** There is no need to decide who "he" meant, whether priming worked, or what the speaker was trying to achieve. Being right about the statement is less important than not getting drawn into it.

**Record if convenient, then get on with your day.** If somebody is in immediate danger, call **999**.
