### Privacy — Knowing When to Stop Trying

Privacy is a worthwhile goal, and taking reasonable precautions to protect it makes sense. However, absolute privacy is extremely difficult to guarantee against a sufficiently capable and determined adversary. Even Apple's Lockdown Mode, designed to protect against sophisticated cyberattacks, cannot guarantee complete security. Internet-connected devices with microphones are potentially vulnerable to compromise, while technologies such as Wi-Fi sensing and millimetre-wave radar can, under suitable conditions, detect physical activity without conventional cameras or microphones. Some of these techniques can operate without leaving obvious traces on the devices being observed. Their practical capabilities vary considerably, and their existence does not mean they are being used against you.

In my experience, **the harder I tried to achieve privacy, the more stressful it became whenever I felt that privacy had been compromised.** I found myself investing increasing amounts of time and effort into precautions without achieving anything whatsoever. Eventually, I stopped trying to make my environment completely private, and my stress levels fell considerably. I realised that trying to defend against every conceivable technical capability was itself becoming a substantial burden. The harder I worked to preserve my privacy, the greater the emotional impact of any perceived intrusion. Giving up the pursuit of perfect privacy was surprisingly liberating.

My recommendation is to take sensible, proportionate precautions. Keep devices updated, physically cover webcams, consider Lockdown Mode where appropriate, and use **passkeys with biometric authentication wherever possible**. SMS-based multifactor authentication is vulnerable to SIM-swapping and other attacks, and should not be mistaken for the strongest available protection, although it is generally better than having no additional authentication. Beyond these precautions, recognise that no technical defence guarantees absolute privacy. As a practical rule, avoid writing or storing anything on a computer that you would be devastated to have disclosed. My personal approach is to **live as though anything I do digitally could potentially become visible, without assuming that anyone is actually watching.** Rather than continually checking whether my privacy has been compromised, I accept the uncertainty and get on with my life. **You do not need perfect privacy to live freely, and you should not allow the pursuit of it to become a prison of its own.**
