# Exaggerated Disgust

**Effects:** Increase shame, Increase distress, Undermine confidence.

**Possible objectives:** Increase the effectiveness of other techniques, Provoke a reaction.

## The pattern

Repeated, conspicuous expressions of disgust are directed at a minor fault, an ordinary behaviour or an accusation that something is dirty or improper. For example, someone might repeatedly exclaim, "Ugh, that's disgusting. He didn't wash his hands properly!" Even when there is no new incident, a similar remark may be made every few minutes.

An exaggerated response can be direct, but it can also be indirect: a speaker may say "he" or "someone" without naming anybody. The listener may then wonder who is being criticised and whether the remark concerns them.

## How it works

Disgust is a powerful social signal. It implies that something is not merely wrong but repellent, and can create embarrassment, a fear of social rejection and an urge to prove one's cleanliness or acceptability. The exaggerated tone may make a small issue feel much more serious than it is.

Repetition adds another layer. Hearing similar expressions again and again can draw attention away from ordinary activities and encourage unnecessary self-monitoring, repeated checking or attempts to defend oneself. An indirect accusation can also invite speculation about what the speaker meant.

A genuine hygiene concern may justify a normal correction. Disgusted remarks, however, do not establish a moral failing, and an overheard comment does not by itself establish whom it concerns or why it was made.

## Response

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**

**If you don't react, you don't give them anything to work with.**
