# Mind Reading

**Effects:** Increase suggestibility, Increase confusion, Undermine confidence.

**Possible objectives:** Increase the effectiveness of other techniques, Provoke a reaction.

## The proposed technique

Is it possible to create the illusion of reading someone's mind by continuously combining LLM prediction, priming and source misattribution.  All that is needed is a hacked mobile phone, and a suitably unethical human or two.

The system uses a large language model (LLM) to predict what someone is likely to think about next, who they are likely to be thinking about, and their approximate emotional state based on their previous statments.  These predictions could be based on extensive information about the individual, potentially including years of recorded speech, their relationships, previous conversations, recurring subjects, emotional associations and broader context.

The LLM produces a list of likely statements, and uses other observations derived from other machine learning tricks to rank their likelihood of being approximately what the victim is going to say next.  The objective is not to predict someone's internal monologue word for word. Predicting the general subject, the person being considered or the emotional meaning of a thought might be sufficiently accurate to create the impression of mind reading.

## How it might work

The technique combines three approaches:

1. **Predicting thoughts and conversations.** Using conversational history, current context and estimated emotional state to predict what the person is likely to discuss or think about next.

2. **Predicting who someone is thinking about.** Using the subject of a conversation, previous associations and emotional state to estimate which person is most likely to be occupying their thoughts. A statement such as "That was for Sarah" could appear to identify someone the individual was thinking about.

3. **Priming and source misattribution.** Introducing words or ideas that may subsequently enter the person's thoughts, then claiming that the individual had already thought or said them. In some circumstances, confusion about the origin of a familiar phrase could make the claim seem convincing.

These three approaches could be used repeatedly in combination.

Crucially, **a failed prediction could still have a useful effect**.

Suppose the model predicts that someone is thinking about Sarah and a someone eg next door shouts, "That was for Sarah."

If the prediction is correct, the individual may feel that somebody has read their mind.  If the prediction is incorrect, hearing Sarah's name may bring her to mind anyway. This will likely influence the person's subsequent thoughts, making later predictions involving Sarah more likely to be correct. If you want an illustration of how this works, try not thinking of a Pink Elephant.  

In this way, prediction and priming could reinforce one another. The prediction changes the very thing the system is attempting to predict, and steers the persons thoughts back to something the model finds easier to predict.  Think of it like a process of constant psychological calibration.

## Combining the techniques: listener-supplied specificity

Source misattribution is a psychological phenomenon in which someone remembers information but incorrectly identifies where it came from. For example, a person might remember something they imagined, dreamed, overheard, or were told and later believe they experienced it directly. This happens because the brain can retain the content of a memory while losing or confusing information about its original source.

A hacked mobile phone be used by an abuser to make a statement, possibly below the threshold of hearing for the victim.  Just because you haven't consciously heard the words, doesn't always prevent priming from happening.  The abuser then shouts something vague about the statement such as "That was about X".  If the victim has not consciouly heard the statement, but priming has been effective then the statement feels like the victims mind has been read. If the victim has consiously heard the statement, "That was about X" appears to refer to the statement.   In the case where the statement made on the mobile phone was not heard and priming was not effective, "That was about X" could appear to be directed at someone else, but would still have the effect of priming the victim and helping calibrate the LLM.

The listener might then fill in what the statement was about themselves. Even if the statement was vague, naming someone they know could bring to mind their own memories, associations and emotions. The listener could supply much of the missing detail, making the statement seem more specific than it actually was.

The process is most effective when it runs continuously throughout the day, using different combinations of source misattribution, priming and predictions. When the model had high confidence of what a victim is thinking, a more specific claim is made. When the confidence is low, a vague statement is made that still primes a person or subject and leaves the listener to fill in the gaps.  Even if the vague statement is incorrect, the victim either assumes it was directed at someone else, or is left feeling defensive "I wasn't thinking about that".  Either way, it primes the victim and callibrates the process.

For example:

- **High confidence:** "That was for Sarah.".  
- **Medium confidence:** "Thats about work"
- **Low confidence:** Just say anything.

All the statements would appear to be directed at the victim or someone else, but in either case the priming effect works. In low confidence, you just say whatever you like loud enough for the victim to hear it.  The priming effect still works even if the statement doesn't appear to be directed at the victim.

The important feature is that a high-confidence statement offers the possibility of a striking apparent success, while a low-confidence statement can introduce a relevant association without making a precise claim. 

The system could then use subsequent speech and behaviour to update its predictions.

## Reinforcement and selective recall

Humans do not necessarily remember successful and unsuccessful predictions equally.  An apparently accurate prediction about a private thought can be surprising and emotionally significant, making it particularly memorable.

An incorrect prediction may be much less memorable, especially when the statement was vague or when the subject subsequently entered the person's thoughts through priming.  If the statements could equally be directed to someone else, they are easily written off as insignificant and forgotten about.

Over time, the individual could remember the striking successes disproportionately while overlooking many of the failures. This creates a cumulative reinforcement effect.  Each apparently successful prediction makes the claimed ability seem more credible. Greater credibility may make subsequent predictions feel more significant, even when the underlying predictive accuracy has not improved.  Repeated exposure to the same subjects and associations might also make them more mentally accessible, potentially influencing subsequent predictions.

The effect does not require a high proportion of predictions to be correct. It requires enough memorable apparent successes to outweigh the subjective significance of the failures.

## Extensive personal information

A large amount of historical information could make the predictions more personalised.

For example, seven years of recorded speech could provide information about an individual's:

- Vocabulary, speech patterns and habitual expressions.
- Friends, family, colleagues and other relationships.
- Recurring conversational subjects and personal concerns.
- Emotional associations with particular people and events.
- Typical conversational sequences and responses.
- Interests, memories and experiences frequently discussed.

This information could be combined with current speech, approximate emotional state, recent events and broader context.

The model could continuously update its estimates of which people, topics and associations were most likely to become relevant next.  Rather than accurately reconstructing private thoughts, the system would be predicting likely associations and occasionally influencing them through its own statements.

## Object-description prediction and priming

The same idea could apply to the names someone might give objects. Imagine someone strokes their beard and another person asks, "Did he just call that his Marx beard?"

A personalised LLM with years of the person's speech, jokes and invented descriptions, plus their film and YouTube viewing history, could generate plausible names. The gesture identifies the object; the model suggests an association. It need not have seen the exact name before.

Like "That was for Sarah", the question could both make a prediction and introduce an association. If the phrase was already in the person's thoughts, the remark might feel accurate. If not, hearing it might bring that phrase to mind. The gesture itself does not reveal an unspoken description, and predicting an exact invented name is considerably harder than suggesting a plausible one.

## Multimodal Sensor Fusion and Real-Time Feedback

The hypothetical system could combine real-time data from every available source to improve its predictions, including microphones, cameras, information obtained from compromised phones, Wi-Fi sensing, mmWave radar and historical information about the individual.

Rather than treating each sensor independently, a multimodal sensor-fusion model could combine the information to estimate the person's current activity, approximate emotional state, conversational context and likely subjects of thought. These estimates could be used to increase or decrease the model's confidence in particular predictions.

**The individual's reactions could also provide a continuous feedback signal.**

For example, microphones could detect changes in speech patterns, cameras could detect facial expressions or movements, and Wi-Fi channel-state information (CSI) or mmWave radar might provide additional measurements of movement, breathing or other physiological activity.

Acoustic cues could provide another set of weak signals. The duration of an audible "hmmmm", hesitation length, pauses, speech rhythm, pitch and breathing patterns might offer clues about cognitive workload or how long someone is deliberating. None of these cues measures the complexity or content of a private thought directly, but they all give useful clues to help the model decide which statement is more likely to be accurate.

If an LLM already had a shortlist of likely statements, a fusion model could use the audio cues to adjust their relative probabilities. Each cue would only need to improve the prediction slightly when combined with the others. Reliable feedback about whether predictions were correct could help identify which signals actually improve accuracy, rather than merely making the model more confident.

The system could compare these observations before and after a statement to estimate whether the individual had reacted. It could then incorporate the feedback into subsequent predictions, potentially improving its personalised model over time.

This introduces another possible feedback mechanism: **prediction, priming, observable reaction and updated prediction could form a continuous loop**, with information from different sensors contributing to the model's confidence.

### Partial speech initiation

Someone might occasionally emit just the beginning of a word they're thinking of without consciously intending to speak — perhaps a plosive or another fragment shorter than a syllable.

An LLM with continuous context could already have a list of likely next words. If a microphone picked up that fragment, it could be another signal for the fusion system, making one prediction more likely than the others. The model wouldn't need to recognise a complete word.

Someone could then confidently ask, "Did he just say X?" If X happened to be correct, it could seem like extraordinary surveillance or mind reading when the explanation might be a cheap parlour trick: prediction, context and a small piece of audible information.

The system uses observable behaviour as probabilistic feedback rather than as a direct measurement of private thoughts.

## Limitations

Suppose an LLM predicts that someone will think about Sarah, a phrase brings Sarah to mind, and a few minutes later the person says something similar to the prediction.

That later statement could help a model predict what the person is likely to say next. It might suggest Sarah was relevant to their thinking at some point, but **it does not reveal when Sarah first came to mind or what they were thinking in between**. They might have thought about several unrelated subjects before returning to her.

The correct predictions will be presented as evidence that the abuser can read your mind, and knows all your internal dialogue.  This is not the case.  They can predict occasionally what you are thinking about, what you might refer to an object as or who you are thinking about.  They might even be able to predict what you are likely to say next.  That does not mean they can read your inner monologue, or hear subvocalisations.

### Selective demonstration of capability

Successful predictions are presented as proof. Failed predictions can be dismissed with "I wasn't trying", while times when no prediction is offered can be explained as "I chose not to tell you" or "Don't tell me what to do". That makes the claim almost impossible to disprove from the demonstrations alone.

This combines **selective reporting** (showing the hits and ignoring the misses) with an **unfalsifiable claim** about a hidden ability.

**Occasionally getting a prediction right does not establish that someone can make that prediction reliably, on demand, or continuously.**

## The cumulative effect

These mechanisms could reinforce one another over an extended period.

**Prediction provides occasional genuine matches.**

**Priming can influence subsequent thoughts towards an otherwise incorrect prediction.**

**Source misattribution can sometimes make an externally introduced idea feel internally generated.**

**Vague statements may lead the listener to supply their own specific meaning.**

**Selective recall makes apparent successes more memorable than ordinary failures.**

**Repetition and reinforcement could make the apparent ability seem increasingly credible.**

None of these mechanisms requires technology capable of directly reading thoughts.

The illusion arises from combining ordinary psychological effects with personalised probabilistic predictions. The more information available about the individual, the more opportunities the model may have to produce contextually appropriate predictions.

The more frequently predictions and suggestions are introduced, the more opportunities there may be for striking coincidences, semantic matches and priming effects.  The result is an increasingly convincing subjective impression that somebody has access to the individual's private thoughts, even when they do not.

### Reading Context and Personal History

If an LLM could see what someone was reading, it would have additional context to help predict what they might think or say next.

For example, I saw the beginning of the word "Royal", which immediately brought someone called Roy to mind.  An LLM that had a list of all my friends as context would potentially be able to predict this, or at least include it among a list of possible inferences with a confidence score.  This could be particularly effective against someone with an extensive social media history, especially someone who has documented their life and had years of written conversations with friends.

That history could provide a personalised model with information about their relationships, memories, interests, vocabulary and personal associations.  The model could combine this information with whatever the person was currently reading to improve its predictions.

**Combined with other signals and continuous feedback, even small improvements in prediction accuracy could make the overall system considerably more effective.**

## Research gap

Prediction from conversational context, semantic priming, source-monitoring errors, confirmation bias and selective recall are all recognised areas of research.  The central unanswered question is whether repeated personalised prediction and priming can produce a sustained difference between actual predictive accuracy and the individual's perceived accuracy. ## Proposed extension: motion-informed sensor fusion

As motion-sensing and action-prediction technology improved, predicted physical actions were fed into the existing fusion model. Video, mmWave radar or other suitable sensors might detect preparatory movement and estimate what someone is about to do before the action is complete.

The model could then use that prediction to reweight its shortlist of candidate interpretations. If two interpretations imply different actions, movement data might help distinguish them and improve the precision of the overall model. Habit and contextual information could contribute to the same comparison.

This would be **probabilistic disambiguation**, not confirmation of a private thought: different thoughts can produce the same movement, and the same thought need not produce any particular movement. The proposed improvement would need to be measured against independently reported intentions, rather than inferred solely from observed actions.

### Heartbeat as a physiological-response signal

A fusion model can also consider heart rate, heart-rate variability and changes from an individual's baseline. Where a stimulus is presented, a subsequent change might help identify the timing of a physiological response, including a response the person did not consciously notice.  Again this is not a way to read your mind, its just a way to add a **physiological-response signal** to the fusion model.

### Possible additional physiological candidates for sensor fusion

Other physiological signals could potentially add useful information to a multimodal fusion model. These are candidates to investigate, not established measurements that a through-wall mmWave system can reliably obtain.

- **Ballistocardiographic movement:** Tiny movements caused by the mechanical forces of the heartbeat. Radar may capture some of this movement and provide information beyond simply counting heartbeats.
- **Pulse waveform morphology:** The shape and amplitude of pulse-related movement may contain information about cardiovascular changes that is lost when the signal is reduced to a heart-rate figure.
- **Beat-to-beat timing and heart-rate variability:** If individual cardiac cycles can be identified reliably, their timing may provide an estimate of heart-rate variability.
- **Cardiorespiratory coupling:** The relationship between breathing and heartbeat, including respiratory sinus arrhythmia, could provide a combined measure of changes in autonomic activity.
- **Pulse transit time:** Timing differences between pulse signals measured at different locations could potentially contribute to estimates of cardiovascular changes. This would require sufficiently reliable measurements at more than one location.
- **Blood-pressure-related changes:** Pulse waveform features and timing might support indirect estimates of blood pressure, although this is considerably less direct than measuring a pulse.
- **Muscle microtremor and tension:** Small involuntary movements, including tremor or changes in muscle tension, could provide additional movement signals where the sensor has sufficient resolution.
- **Swallowing and throat movement:** Swallows and other small throat movements may be detectable as distinct physical events.
- **Vocal-tract and speech-related vibration:** Radar might capture some movement associated with speech production, including vocal activity and articulatory movement.
- **Postural sway and small adjustments:** Subtle changes in balance, posture and body position could provide information about movement or physical state.
- **Startle and freezing responses:** Sudden movement, or a temporary reduction in movement, could be useful event-related signals when considered alongside the other measurements.
- **Gait microstructure:** Changes in walking rhythm, step timing and movement consistency could provide further behavioural information when the person is moving.

Some of these signals are more plausible than others for mmWave radar. Chest movement, cardiac mechanical movement, respiration and larger body movements are the more direct candidates. Estimates of pulse-wave characteristics or beat-to-beat timing depend on signal quality and validation. Blood pressure, pulse transit time and peripheral vasoconstriction are more indirect: they should not be treated as measurements that mmWave radar can simply read through a wall. Vasoconstriction in particular is usually assessed through changes in peripheral blood flow or skin blood volume, which may require a different sensing modality.

The useful question for a fusion model would be whether any of these signals adds predictive information beyond the signals already available. Each candidate would need to be tested separately and then in combination, against an independent reference measurement. A signal that changes after a stimulus may help locate the timing of a response, but it does not by itself identify the cause of that response.

## Continuous priming as a possible feedback mechanism

Another possible feedback mechanism for the hypothetical fusion system would be to combine the internal monologue trick with continuous prediction and priming. A model could produce its best guesses at the next word or association and introduce those guesses as very quiet speech, potentially timed with footsteps or other everyday sounds to make the speech less noticeable.

If a suggestion happened to match a person's existing thought, it might appear unusually accurate. If it did not match, hearing the suggestion might still bring the word or a related idea to mind. A model could then use subsequent observable speech, behaviour or physiological responses to adjust its next suggestions. This would allow several feedback mechanisms to operate together, with each contributing a possible weak signal to the same model.

There is an important distinction between improving predictions and influencing what happens next. A response following a quietly spoken word would not establish that the person was already thinking of that word before it was played. The loop might calibrate which prompts influence later responses without accurately identifying unspoken thoughts. Whether synchronising quiet words with footsteps would make this process effective remains untested.
