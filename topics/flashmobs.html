Supermarket Flashmobs — Group Intimidation and Public Provocation

Experience: Over an extended period, I experienced repeated incidents in supermarkets involving strangers making unusual, apparently personal or accusatory remarks within earshot. Sometimes the remarks were shouted from another aisle; on other occasions they were spoken quietly as someone passed me. Some appeared to refer to subjects that had previously featured in other disturbing experiences.

I came to refer to these incidents as "flashmobs" because of my impression that multiple people were participating in a similar pattern of behaviour. The incidents were not confined to customers. I also experienced behaviour from supermarket employees that I considered inappropriate. On one occasion, a manager at a supermarket walked past me and quietly said, "You are not welcome here." I was subsequently informed that I had been banned from the store.

Possible method: An organised version of this technique would involve several individuals making provocative, insulting or apparently incriminating remarks around a target in an ordinary public setting. Participants would not necessarily need to approach the target directly. Remarks could be made while passing, from adjoining aisles or during otherwise ordinary interactions, making it difficult to determine whether they were directed at the individual.

The appearance of multiple participants would be central to the effect. A series of apparently independent encounters could create the impression of widespread hostility or knowledge of private information, even without anyone making a direct accusation.

Psychological mechanism: The technique would exploit social pressure, ambiguity, reputational anxiety and the tendency to identify patterns in repeated experiences. Public environments offer numerous opportunities for overheard conversations, making it difficult to distinguish deliberate remarks from coincidences or unrelated speech.

The resulting uncertainty can itself become distressing. An individual may begin paying excessive attention to strangers' conversations, anticipating further incidents or feeling uncomfortable in otherwise ordinary public places.

Potential objective: If deliberately organised, the objective might be to intimidate, humiliate, socially isolate or provoke an individual while leaving little evidence of misconduct by any single participant. A further objective could be to encourage the individual to confront strangers or make sweeping accusations against people whose involvement cannot be established.

Defence: The most effective response is to continue shopping normally, avoid confrontations and treat ambiguous remarks as irrelevant unless someone clearly addresses you. Where an identifiable person makes a direct threat or behaves inappropriately, record the specific words and circumstances rather than attempting to establish connections between unrelated encounters.

The central principle is to remain guided by direct observations rather than apparent patterns. Strangers' remarks do not require a response, and ordinary activities should not be abandoned because of ambiguous social interactions.

Questions this chapter explores

  • Why can group behaviour feel more convincing than an individual remark?
  • How can I avoid drawing conclusions about strangers' intentions from a crowd?
