**False Endings — Manipulated Relief and Withdrawal of Reassurance**

**Technique:** False endings involve announcing that an individual has finally complied with the demands imposed upon them and that the abuse or punishment will now stop, only to withdraw that promise almost immediately. A trivial, arbitrary or entirely unrelated action is then presented as a breach of the agreement: *“You did X, deal’s off!”* The supposed agreement may never have been genuinely negotiated, and the conditions for maintaining it may be deliberately vague or impossible to satisfy.

**Psychological objective:** The purpose is to repeatedly create hope and relief before abruptly taking them away. By allowing the individual to believe that an unpleasant experience has finally ended, the abuser makes its sudden resumption particularly distressing. This can encourage the individual to pursue compliance more desperately, scrutinise their own behaviour for supposed mistakes and accept increasingly unreasonable demands in the hope of securing a permanent ending. It also shifts responsibility for the continuation of the abuse onto the individual, rather than the person choosing to continue it.

**Psychological mechanism:** The technique exploits *intermittent reinforcement*, *relief conditioning*, *expectation violation* and *learned helplessness*. The unpredictable alternation between promised relief and renewed punishment can make the possibility of an ending unusually compelling. Repeated cycles may also undermine the individual's confidence in their ability to influence outcomes through reasonable behaviour. The sudden withdrawal of reassurance can produce a sharper emotional reaction than uninterrupted hostility because it follows a period of anticipated safety.

**An important feature is that the ending is presented as something the individual must continually earn, rather than something the abuser is responsible for delivering.** In combination with impossible compliance, this creates a self-perpetuating cycle: compliance is declared successful, relief is promised, a trivial violation is invented, and the abuse resumes. The practical defence is to avoid treating unilateral promises, arbitrary conditions or supposed breaches as legitimate obligations.
