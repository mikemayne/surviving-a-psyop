Experience: An ongoing voice-like monologue resembling my own inner dialogue

I experienced a recurring, sometimes seemingly continuous stream of quiet words and sentences that sounded like a spoken version of my own internal monologue. At times the voice resembled mine; at other times it sounded like someone else communicating with me. Some phrases were close to things I might naturally say, or seemed to fit what I was doing or thinking about, while others were less relevant. I could hear the words, but I did not experience myself as speaking them or deliberately thinking them. What made this particularly strange was the combination of familiar-sounding language, apparent relevance and a sound source that was sometimes very difficult to locate.

The speech-like experience could occur alongside everyday sounds, including footsteps, rustling or sounds I noticed while lying with an ear against a pillow. Sometimes it seemed unusually close or almost internal, without becoming my own thought. In my experience it was associated with having my phone or another possible loudspeaker present; I did not notice it when neither was present. The repeated intrusions could interrupt my attention and make it feel as though a running commentary was following my activities.

This can be followed by vocal reactions by people who claim to have heard you say the thing you heard. Often this will be a slur of some kind directed at them, followed by them threatening you in some way.

After I realised what was going on, this was on more than one occasion done at more than one volume level to try to re-establish that the quieter level was indeed the inner-voice and while the higher level was my phone.

Technical explanation

I believe a way to acheive the proposed effect would be to combine language generation with speech synthesis and playback. Under the hypothetical assumption of a fully compromised smartphone, software could use accessible conversational history, written language, recent activity or spoken context to generate a succession of plausible phrases. A general-purpose language model could do much of this with instructions and retrieved examples of an individual's language; extensive personal fine-tuning would not necessarily be required. Text-to-speech conditioned on a sample of the person's voice could turn the generated text into familiar-sounding audio, played through a nearby speaker. This would predict plausible language from observable context, not read unspoken thoughts. [1, 2]

Psychoacoustically, a faint or band-limited voice near other everyday sounds may sometimes be audible but difficult to locate. Clothing, the position of a nearby speaker, competing sound and an ear pressed against a pillow can change the available cues. Context can also affect how ambiguous speech is understood. These principles do not establish that a recording was present or that a voice would necessarily seem internal under particular conditions. [3]

Psychologically, repeated language that resembles a person's own style could feel unusually personal even if much of it was only a prediction. Semantic priming and expectation may make related words or subjects more noticeable, while uncertainty over the origin of a familiar-sounding voice can make the experience more intrusive. Importantly, recognising a sound as resembling one's own voice, experiencing it as inside the head and feeling that one intentionally thought or spoke the words are distinct things. The experience described here preserves that distinction. [4, 5]

The main AI/ML principles to examine are language modelling, personalised contextual retrieval, voice-conditioned text-to-speech and continuous generation. The main psychoacoustic principles are auditory masking, source localisation, auditory scene analysis and perceptual restoration. The main psychological principles are semantic priming, predictive perception, attention capture, source attribution and the distinction between familiar voice identity and agency. The integrated system's actual effectiveness would have to be tested independently; the existence of these individual components does not determine the origin of the experience.
