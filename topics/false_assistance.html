False Assistance: Control Disguised as Concern

One form of psychological manipulation involves presenting harmful, humiliating or controlling behaviour as though it were intended to help the person experiencing it.

The person responsible may describe their behaviour as an intervention, a form of treatment, a necessary lesson or an attempt to protect somebody from themselves.

They may also imply that the unpleasant treatment will stop as soon as the person cooperates, accepts the supposed help or demonstrates that they have learned their lesson.

The trick is to make someone believe that resisting mistreatment means rejecting help, and that complying with unreasonable demands is the only way to make it stop.

How It Works

Imagine somebody repeatedly insulting, intimidating or humiliating another person while insisting:

  • "We're only doing this because we care about you."
  • "One day you'll understand why this was necessary."
  • "This is an intervention for your own good."
  • "We're trying to help you become a better person."
  • "Your resistance proves you need our help."
  • "You should be grateful for what we're doing."

These statements attempt to redefine the relationship between the person exercising control and the person experiencing it.

Instead of being accountable for their actions, the person exercising control claims to be acting as a benefactor.

The recipient is encouraged to see reasonable objections as evidence of their own shortcomings.

The Intervention Pretext

The word "intervention" can lend an appearance of legitimacy to behaviour that would otherwise be recognised as unacceptable.

A genuine intervention should have a legitimate purpose, appropriate safeguards and some means of accountability.

Simply calling something an intervention does not make it one.

For example, someone might claim that deliberate humiliation is necessary to correct another person's behaviour, or that intimidation is intended to encourage them to seek help.

These claims should not be accepted merely because they are presented in the language of care or treatment.

The claimed purpose of an action does not excuse the action itself.

Weaponised Concern

False assistance can be particularly effective when somebody expresses apparent sympathy while simultaneously attempting to control another person's decisions.

For example:

"We're worried about you. That's why you need to do exactly what we say."

Or:

"If you won't accept our help, you're clearly not thinking rationally."

The second statement creates a logical trap. Accepting the assistance is treated as evidence of good judgement, while rejecting it is treated as evidence that the assistance is necessary.

It becomes impossible to disagree without apparently confirming the accusation.

This is closely related to the double binds described in the chapter on logic traps.

The Promise of Relief

False assistance may also imply that unpleasant treatment will end once the person accepts the supposed help, acknowledges a problem or complies with the demands being made.

For example:

  • "The sooner you cooperate, the sooner this will be over."
  • "We're only continuing because you're resisting."
  • "Once you've learned your lesson, we can stop."
  • "We're trying to help you, but you have to let us."
  • "You can put an end to all this by doing what we ask."

This creates a powerful incentive to comply. The person may begin to believe that they are responsible for the continuation of their own mistreatment, rather than recognising that whoever is engaging in the behaviour remains responsible for their actions.

It also creates the possibility of a moving target. Once one demand has been satisfied, another condition may be introduced, keeping the person engaged in an endless attempt to obtain relief.

The person may become increasingly willing to make concessions that they would ordinarily reject, simply because each concession appears to offer an opportunity to end the situation.

The important distinction is between genuine assistance, which has clear purposes and boundaries, and coercive behaviour that makes relief conditional on obedience.

Do not assume that complying with unreasonable demands will bring unwanted treatment to an end.

Equally, do not accept responsibility for somebody else's decision to mistreat you simply because they claim your resistance is causing it.

You do not have to earn the right to be treated with dignity.

Creating Dependency

Another possible objective is to convince somebody that they cannot function independently.

The person offering supposed assistance may repeatedly undermine their confidence, question their judgement or suggest that ordinary decisions require permission.

They may also imply that protection, reassurance or an end to unpleasant treatment depends on continued cooperation.

This can encourage the person to look to the very individual exercising control for reassurance, approval or relief.

When assistance is conditional on surrendering unreasonable amounts of personal autonomy, it deserves scrutiny.

Help should generally increase your ability to make informed decisions, not create unnecessary dependence on the person offering it.

How to Recognise Genuine Assistance

Not every difficult intervention is manipulative. Healthcare professionals, friends and family sometimes need to raise uncomfortable concerns, and legitimate emergency interventions may sometimes involve restrictions.

Rather than trying to guess somebody's motives, consider their actions.

Genuine assistance should ordinarily involve an identifiable purpose, reasonable explanations, respect for your dignity and opportunities to ask questions.

Where restrictions are necessary, they should be lawful, proportionate, appropriately authorised and subject to safeguards.

Someone acting in good faith should ordinarily be willing to explain what they are doing, what authority they have and how concerns can be reviewed.

Pay particular attention to whether the assistance has a clear objective and an identifiable endpoint, or whether the conditions for ending it repeatedly change.

How to Respond

You do not need to determine whether somebody genuinely believes they are helping you.

You can judge the proposed action on its merits.

Ask yourself whether the assistance is wanted, appropriate, proportionate and coming from somebody with a legitimate reason to offer it.

Where possible:

  • Ask for a clear explanation of what is being proposed.
  • Distinguish an offer of help from a demand for compliance.
  • Do not make unreasonable concessions simply because somebody promises that doing so will end unpleasant treatment.
  • Seek independent advice if your rights or welfare are affected.
  • Establish boundaries calmly and clearly.
  • Use appropriate complaints or review procedures where necessary.

Avoid arguing about whether somebody secretly cares about you. Their motives may be impossible to establish, and you don't need to establish them to decide whether their behaviour is acceptable.

If someone is behaving abusively, disengage where it is safe and practical to do so. You do not need to convince them that their behaviour is wrong before protecting your own interests.

The Important Principle

Concern is not authority. Good intentions, even when genuine, do not automatically justify harmful or controlling behaviour.

The promise that mistreatment will stop if you cooperate is not a reason to surrender your judgement or accept unreasonable demands.

You can acknowledge somebody's stated concern without agreeing with their conclusions or complying with their demands.

Evaluate assistance by what is actually being offered, how it is delivered and whether it respects your rights and dignity — not simply by the benevolent language used to describe it.

You are allowed to accept help. You are allowed to question it. And, subject to legitimate legal obligations, you are allowed to refuse it.

You are not responsible for somebody else's mistreatment of you, and you should not have to comply with unreasonable demands to make it stop.
