Delayed Responses from Organisations

When this chapter might help

This chapter may be useful if you're thinking:

  • "Why won't they reply to me?"
  • "I'm just going to follow up now."
  • "They're ignoring my complaint."
  • "Should I chase them again?"
  • "I've been waiting weeks for an answer."
  • "It's been years. Is it too late to complain?"

If you're frustrated by an organisation's lack of response, read this chapter before deciding what to do next.

Delayed responses from organisations are frustrating, but they can also be useful. They give you a chance to organise your thoughts, become more grounded, and regain your composure.

The way out is not to become aggressive or shout at people. You cannot force people to listen to you, and trying to do so is counterproductive.

Use the time to calmly but firmly exercise your rights. Subject Access Requests (SARs) can help you obtain historical personal information held about you, including records about your treatment and contact with organisations. You can also submit formal complaints.

If you can, make a complaint relatively quickly. It is generally easier to investigate events while records are available and people's memories are fresh. But if you haven't felt able to complain for months, or even several years, it can still be worth doing. Be polite and respectful, and explain the circumstances that prevented you from raising your complaint sooner. Some complaint processes have time limits, so check the rules and ask for a late complaint to be considered where appropriate.

Where possible, use email and keep copies of what you send and receive. If you use an email account connected to ChatGPT, it can help you organise correspondence and prepare polite follow-ups. Review drafts yourself before sending them, and be thoughtful about sharing sensitive information.

Give people a reasonable amount of time to respond. A week is a sensible interval before following up on an ordinary enquiry. Formal complaints and Subject Access Requests may have longer published or statutory response times, so check those and don't mistake a normal processing period for a failure to respond.

Use delays to become calmer, clearer and better organised. Be polite, persistent and firm. You will have a much easier time getting people to consider your concerns if you are composed and stick to the facts.

For further guidance on recording events, raising concerns and following up, see Ready to Report?.
