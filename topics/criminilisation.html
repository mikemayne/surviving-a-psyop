Criminalisation of the Mundane

Objective: To make an individual feel that ordinary, harmless behaviour is inherently criminal, immoral or shameful, creating a persistent sense of guilt, vulnerability and fear of accusation.

How the technique works

The technique involves repeatedly interpreting completely innocent actions as evidence of serious wrongdoing, particularly sexual offences, hate crimes or other socially stigmatised behaviour.

For example, hanging underwear out to dry might be characterised as a sexual offence, an innocuous choice of words might be described as racist or homophobic, or an ordinary physical movement might be presented as evidence of inappropriate sexual intent.

The accusations need not be remotely plausible. Their purpose is not necessarily to convince an independent observer that a crime has occurred, but to make the targeted individual feel that almost anything they do can be used against them.

Over time, the individual may begin to anticipate accusations before they occur, scrutinising their own behaviour and attempting to avoid even the most trivial opportunities for malicious interpretation.

Psychological mechanisms

The technique combines several established forms of psychological manipulation:

  • Malicious reframing: Assigning criminal or immoral significance to actions that have an entirely innocent explanation.
  • Shame conditioning: Repeatedly associating ordinary behaviour with particularly stigmatised forms of wrongdoing, encouraging feelings of embarrassment, guilt or disgust.
  • Impossible compliance: Creating an environment in which there is no reliable way to behave correctly because the standards of acceptable conduct are arbitrary and constantly changing.
  • Moral intimidation: Exploiting the seriousness of accusations involving sexual misconduct, racism or other forms of prejudice to make the individual reluctant to challenge them.
  • Hypervigilance: Encouraging constant self-monitoring, potentially making even routine activities mentally exhausting.
  • Gaslighting: Where the accusations are accompanied by repeated attempts to persuade the individual that their own understanding of innocent behaviour is fundamentally unreliable.

Intended psychological effect

The cumulative objective is to undermine the individual's confidence in their own judgement and make them feel perpetually vulnerable to accusation.

A particularly damaging feature is that the individual may begin modifying entirely harmless behaviour to avoid further accusations. This creates an expanding set of self-imposed restrictions, despite there being no legitimate reason for the original behaviour to change.

The technique can also exploit the individual's desire to be a decent, law-abiding person. Someone who takes accusations of sexual misconduct or prejudice seriously may initially devote considerable effort to understanding and avoiding the supposed offences.

Because the accusations are arbitrary, however, no amount of reasonable compliance can guarantee that they will stop.

Recognising and resisting the technique

The important distinction is between an actual legal or ethical standard and an accusation that merely invokes one.

An ordinary action does not become criminal simply because somebody chooses to describe it in criminal terms. Nor does the intensity, repetition or apparent moral certainty of an accusation establish its validity.

The most effective response is to assess the behaviour against ordinary, consistent standards rather than accepting the accuser's interpretation. Where there is no reasonable basis for the accusation, there is no need to alter harmless behaviour or engage in endless attempts to prove one's innocence.

The central principle is simple: an accusation is not an offence, and ordinary behaviour does not become wrongdoing merely because someone insists that it is.
