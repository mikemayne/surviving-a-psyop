Name and Shame — Threatened Reputational Destruction

**Objective:** To intimidate, control or coerce an individual by threatening to make damaging accusations about them, either publicly or privately to friends, family, colleagues or other people whose opinions matter to them. The objective may be to induce compliance, discourage complaints, damage relationships or create fear of social rejection and reputational harm.

**Method:** The technique involves threatening to reveal alleged wrongdoing, embarrassing information or accusations of criminal or morally reprehensible behaviour. Statements such as "We'll tell everyone what you've done", "Your friends will find out" or "We'll name and shame you" may be used to suggest that the individual faces serious social consequences unless they behave as demanded.

The accusations need not be true to be intimidating. Threats involving highly stigmatised offences, particularly sexual offences, can be especially distressing because the individual may fear that some recipients will believe the accusations without examining the evidence.

A variation involves threatening to contact selected people privately rather than making a public announcement. This may be more difficult for the individual to discover or challenge, particularly when the alleged accusations are communicated through private conversations.

**Psychological mechanism:** Name and shame intimidation exploits fear of rejection, reputational anxiety, shame and the importance of personal relationships. The individual may fear losing friendships, employment, family relationships or standing within their community.

The technique can create an artificial dilemma in which the individual feels compelled to choose between complying with unreasonable demands and risking potentially devastating accusations.

It may also exploit anticipatory anxiety: the threat of future humiliation can be used repeatedly without the intimidator ever carrying it out. Uncertainty about who might be contacted, what they might be told and whether they would believe it can become a source of continuing pressure.

**Exploitation:** The threatened disclosure may be used to discourage an individual from reporting misconduct, seeking legal advice, speaking openly about their experiences or refusing demands.

A particularly coercive variation involves threatening to tell the individual's family or friends that they have committed a serious crime, while simultaneously claiming that the accusations will remain private if the individual complies.

The technique may also be combined with accusation baiting or public shaming. An individual who reacts angrily or makes unsupported counteraccusations may find those reactions subsequently used to reinforce the original allegations.

Where accusations are actually communicated, the resulting damage may include strained relationships, social isolation, workplace difficulties and the need to respond to rumours that the individual did not know were circulating.

**Defence:** Do not allow threats of reputational damage to dictate behaviour or induce compliance with unreasonable demands. Distinguish between an accusation and evidence supporting it, and avoid responding with threats or speculative counteraccusations.

Preserve direct threats where possible, including their exact wording and any written communications. Where there is a credible risk of defamatory allegations, harassment, blackmail or malicious communications, seek appropriate independent legal advice.

There is no obligation to pre-emptively defend oneself against rumours that may never be circulated, nor to confront friends or family about accusations they may never have heard.

The central principle is to **refuse to exchange personal autonomy or silence for a promise of reputational protection. A threatened accusation is not proof of wrongdoing, and the possibility of public embarrassment does not give another person legitimate authority over your behaviour.**
