# Stockholm Syndrome — Attachment Under Threat

## The Pattern

Stockholm syndrome is a popular label for situations in which someone who has been threatened, confined or controlled appears to feel sympathy, gratitude, loyalty or protectiveness towards the person responsible. The term arose after a hostage-taking in Stockholm in 1973.

Despite its familiar name, Stockholm syndrome is not a recognised psychiatric diagnosis. There are no agreed diagnostic criteria, and research does not establish it as a distinct condition. Some researchers prefer the term **appeasement** for behaviour that helps somebody survive a threatening situation.

## Why It Can Happen

When one person controls another's immediate safety, keeping that person calm may seem essential. Someone under threat might monitor their moods, agree with them, avoid upsetting them or pay close attention to occasional acts of kindness.

Relief is powerful. If a person who has behaved threateningly becomes friendly, offers reassurance or allows an ordinary freedom, the change may feel enormous. In a frightening environment, the person who can end the immediate distress may start to feel like a source of safety, even when they are also responsible for the danger.

In longer-term abusive relationships, fear, attachment, practical dependence, hope and affection can coexist. This does not mean the person approves of mistreatment or freely chooses it. Nor does everyone exposed to coercion develop these feelings.

## Recognising the Pattern

Possible examples include defending somebody who has caused harm, minimising their behaviour, feeling unusually grateful for basic kindness, or worrying more about getting them into trouble than about one's own treatment.

But these reactions are not proof of Stockholm syndrome or even proof of abuse. Apparent loyalty might reflect fear of retaliation, a deliberate attempt to stay safe, an existing emotional relationship, limited alternatives or circumstances an outsider does not know about. Cooperation is not necessarily affection, and affection is not necessarily consent.

The useful question is not whether a label fits. It is whether the relationship allows **safety, independent judgement, reasonable boundaries and freedom to seek help**.

## Further considerations
Judge behaviour over time rather than by isolated moments of warmth or hostility. Someone being kind occasionally does not excuse threats, humiliation or unreasonable control. Equally, feeling relieved, grateful or attached does not create an obligation to obey.

### Don't Try to Win Them Over

If somebody is threatening, humiliating or pressuring you, **do not try to win them over with charm, prove their accusations false by revealing intimate details, or treat the exchange like an unusual therapy session.** You do not have to earn fair treatment by explaining your private life to someone who is behaving coercively.

Be careful what you disclose. Personal statements can be taken out of context, repeated to turn others against you, used to apply further pressure, or exploited for blackmail. Information that is recorded and processed could also be used for automated profiling or machine-learning purposes, although that should not be assumed without evidence of recording or processing. The point is to avoid giving unnecessary personal information to someone whose intentions or handling of it you cannot trust.

Keep any necessary exchanges brief and factual. Do not argue endlessly about accusations or offer more and more personal information in the hope of finally being believed. Save sensitive discussions for people you independently trust, including appropriately qualified professionals who have clear duties of confidentiality.

If a relationship feels controlling, talk privately with someone trustworthy and independent. Consider what happens when you disagree, ask for space or seek outside support. If leaving or confronting the person could be dangerous, prioritise a safe plan and specialist support rather than acting under pressure.

If you are supporting somebody else, avoid calling them irrational, accusing them of loving an abuser, or demanding immediate decisions. Listen, respect their judgement and help them explore practical choices safely.

**The central principle:** A person's response to fear may help them cope or survive. It should not be mistaken for approval of mistreatment, and it should not be used to blame them for what happened.

## Response

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**

**If you don't react, you don't give them anything to work with.**
