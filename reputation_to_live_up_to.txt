# Giving Someone a Reputation to Live Up To

**Effects:** Create pressure to fit an assigned identity.

**Possible objectives:** Influence behaviour.

In *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, Dale Carnegie describes a technique called "Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to."

The principle is simple: people are often motivated to behave consistently with the identity or reputation others assign to them.

Used positively, this can encourage confidence, kindness and better behaviour. Someone described as trustworthy, capable or generous may feel motivated to demonstrate those qualities.

The same principle can also be used manipulatively.

Examples include:

- "You're a good person. You wouldn't refuse to help."
- "You're not the sort of person who complains."
- "You've always been loyal. You wouldn't tell anyone about this."
- "You're intelligent enough to understand why this is necessary."

The technique works by creating an expectation and encouraging the individual to meet it. Rather than making a direct demand, the speaker associates a particular action with an appealing identity.

A manipulative variation can make refusing a request feel like betraying your own character. Repeatedly assigning someone a negative reputation can also influence their confidence and behaviour, although this is a related form of labelling rather than Carnegie's original positive technique.

## Exaggerating Someone's Abilities

Another variation involves repeatedly exaggerating someone's intelligence, abilities or importance, encouraging them to believe they possess exceptional talents, knowledge or capabilities that other people lack.

Excessive praise and reinforcement of unrealistic beliefs can encourage overconfidence, poor judgement and increasingly ambitious or risky decisions. In someone vulnerable to mania, such messages could potentially reinforce grandiose thinking, although praise alone is not established as a cause of mania.

The important distinction is between genuine encouragement and claims that encourage someone to abandon realistic assessments of their abilities.

**Your reputation does not oblige you to behave according to somebody else's expectations.**

Judge requests on their merits. Being kind doesn't require you to agree with everything. Being loyal doesn't require secrecy. Being reasonable doesn't mean surrendering your judgement. And being told you have extraordinary abilities doesn't make those claims true.

## Recommended Reading

*How to Win Friends and Influence People* by Dale Carnegie.

This is an excellent introduction to interpersonal influence, communication and persuasion. The book is primarily concerned with building positive relationships rather than manipulation, but understanding its principles can also help you recognise when ordinary techniques of influence are being used to pressure you.

Learning how persuasion works can make it easier to recognise a provocative remark without answering it. Ignore the provocation and carry on with your day.

## Response

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**

**If you don't react, you don't give them anything to work with.**
