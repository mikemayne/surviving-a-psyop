# Cold Reading — Creating the Illusion of Knowing the Unknowable

Cold reading is a psychological technique used to create the impression that someone possesses knowledge about another person that they could not reasonably have obtained. It is commonly associated with stage mentalists, fortune tellers and fraudulent psychics, but the same principles can be used in social manipulation, intimidation and confidence tricks.

The technique relies on making plausible guesses, observing reactions and gradually refining statements until they appear remarkably accurate. A skilled cold reader may seem to know someone's private thoughts, memories, relationships or personal circumstances without having any special access to that information.

### How It Works

Cold reading exploits several psychological mechanisms:

- **High-probability guesses:** Making statements likely to apply to many people, particularly when their age, circumstances or emotional state are known.
- **The Barnum effect:** Presenting vague or broadly applicable descriptions as though they reveal something uniquely personal.
- **Fishing for information:** Making ambiguous statements or asking leading questions that encourage the target to reveal details without recognising that they are supplying the information.
- **Reading reactions:** Observing facial expressions, hesitation, body language and changes in tone to determine which guesses are accurate.
- **Shotgunning:** Making numerous predictions or suggestions, knowing that some will inevitably be correct.
- **Selective reinforcement:** Drawing attention to successful guesses while ignoring, dismissing or reinterpreting unsuccessful ones.
- **Retrospective interpretation:** Making ambiguous statements that can later be presented as accurate predictions once additional information becomes available.

### Technology and Cold Reading

Modern technology can substantially improve the apparent accuracy of cold reading.

Information obtained from social media, public records, previous conversations or compromised accounts can be used to construct detailed profiles of individuals. This is sometimes called *hot reading* when the information has been obtained in advance.

Machine learning models can also analyse language, behavioural patterns and personal information to generate predictions about what someone might think, feel or say in a particular situation.

Large language models are especially useful for generating plausible statements based on an individual's known interests, vocabulary, experiences and circumstances. These predictions may sometimes appear strikingly personal without requiring any direct access to the individual's thoughts.

However, predicting what someone might think is not the same as observing what they actually think. Even sophisticated models can produce convincing but entirely incorrect predictions.

### Cold Reading Using a Compromised Phone

A particularly powerful hypothetical extension of cold reading involves a compromised smartphone capable of supplying microphone or camera information to a third party.

If malicious software gains sufficient access to a device, it may be possible to capture audio, images or video without the owner's informed consent. The precise capabilities depend on the operating system, device security, permissions and level of compromise.

This information could substantially improve the accuracy of predictions about the person's circumstances, activities and likely reactions.

**Microphone Information**

A microphone can reveal considerably more than the words someone speaks.

Modern audio-processing systems can potentially extract:

- **Speech content:** Automatic speech recognition can transcribe conversations, including remarks that the speaker might consider private.
- **Speaker identification:** Voice characteristics can sometimes help distinguish known speakers and associate conversations with particular individuals.
- **Activity recognition:** Sounds such as typing, running water, footsteps, television programmes or household appliances can provide clues about someone's activities.
- **Emotional and behavioural cues:** Changes in speech rate, volume, hesitation and vocal characteristics may provide imperfect clues about stress, excitement or fatigue.
- **Environmental context:** Background noises may help distinguish environments and activities.
- **Conversation patterns:** Repeated observations can reveal common topics, routines, relationships and likely responses.

These capabilities depend heavily on recording quality, microphone placement and background noise. A distant microphone in a noisy room cannot reliably recover arbitrary whispered or unspoken language, and ordinary microphones cannot detect someone's internal monologue.

**Camera Information**

A compromised camera could provide additional information, provided it has a useful view of the person or their surroundings.

Computer vision systems can analyse:

- **Facial movements:** Expressions, eye movements and changes in facial muscle activity, although these cannot reliably reveal specific thoughts.
- **Body language:** Posture, gestures and movements that may indicate activities or behavioural responses.
- **Object recognition:** Identifying visible objects, clothing, books, food or other items in the environment.
- **Activity recognition:** Recognising visible activities such as reading, exercising, eating or using another device.
- **Behavioural patterns:** Identifying repeated movements, routines and responses over time.

The effectiveness of these techniques depends on lighting, image resolution, camera orientation and whether the relevant activity is actually visible.

Facial-expression recognition can identify observable movements, but assigning precise emotions or mental states to those movements is substantially less reliable. A camera cannot directly reveal memories, private thoughts or the words of an internal monologue.

**Combining Information with Machine Learning**

The most interesting technical possibility is the combination of observations with a predictive model of the individual.

For example, a system might combine:

1. Recently overheard conversations.
2. Visible activities and objects.
3. Previously observed behavioural patterns.
4. Information about the person's interests and circumstances.
5. Predictions generated by a language model.

The resulting system could generate contextually appropriate guesses about what the person is likely to say, do or consider next.

A model might predict that someone reading a particular news article is thinking about the subject of that article, or that someone who has just finished a conversation is likely to continue considering its contents.

These are probabilistic inferences, not direct measurements of thought. Their accuracy would vary considerably, and impressive individual predictions would not establish that the system consistently knows someone's mental state.

**Creating the Impression of Mind Reading**

If someone had access to such information, they could potentially combine it with conventional cold-reading techniques to create a misleading impression of extraordinary knowledge.

For example, they might make a series of guesses about someone's recent activities or concerns, emphasise accurate statements, and disregard incorrect ones.

They might also introduce a subject into a conversation and subsequently make predictions about the person's reactions to that subject.

The psychological effect could be strengthened by claiming that the information was obtained through an extraordinary capability rather than ordinary observation, prior knowledge or prediction.

However, there is an important distinction between three different capabilities:

- **Knowing:** Obtaining information directly from an observable source, such as an audible conversation.
- **Predicting:** Inferring probable behaviour or mental content from available information.
- **Influencing:** Introducing information that may affect what someone subsequently thinks about or does.

These capabilities can overlap, but none amounts to unrestricted access to another person's mind.

### Psychological Effects

Cold reading can become particularly unsettling when someone repeatedly appears to know private information.

A person may begin to question whether their conversations are being monitored, whether their private thoughts are somehow accessible, or whether someone possesses extraordinary technological capabilities.

This uncertainty can encourage the target to search for increasingly elaborate explanations, particularly if apparently accurate guesses are combined with intimidation, suggestion or claims of secret knowledge.

The resulting preoccupation can itself become disruptive, regardless of how the apparent knowledge was obtained.

### Further considerations
**You do not need to explain how someone obtained apparently private information to decide how to respond.**

Avoid treating accurate guesses as proof of extraordinary capabilities. Remember that coincidences, prior knowledge, ordinary observation and statistical predictions can produce surprisingly convincing results.

Do not become drawn into repeated tests intended to establish what another person knows. Such interactions can encourage further speculation and give the other person additional information about your reactions.

If you have specific indications that a device has been compromised, ordinary cybersecurity precautions are appropriate: update its software, review application permissions and account access, and seek competent technical assistance where necessary. An apparently accurate guess, by itself, is not a reliable indicator of device compromise.

If someone makes threats, demands or attempts to coerce you, do not reply or debate their claimed abilities. Record the words if convenient, and report credible threats to the police through the appropriate channel. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 999.

**The central principle is simple: someone appearing to know something about you does not give them authority over you, and you are under no obligation to comply with their demands.**

Learning from the Target's Responses

Cold reading can also function as a process of information gathering. Every response to a guess, whether verbal or behavioural, may provide additional information about the individual. Modern machine learning systems could potentially accumulate this feedback to refine a personalised model of someone's interests, behaviour and likely responses. This need not involve repeatedly retraining an entire language model; information from previous interactions can simply be incorporated into the context used to generate subsequent predictions. Over time, such a system might produce increasingly convincing demonstrations of apparent personal knowledge. However, reactions are ambiguous, and predicting someone's responses is not equivalent to reading their thoughts.

## Research gap

General cold-reading mechanisms are well described. The accuracy of any particular apparent prediction depends on specificity, context and chance; blinded prospective tests can distinguish repeatable performance from selective recall.

## Response

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**

**If you don't react, you don't give them anything to work with.**
