# Fake Shock: Feigned Sympathy

**Effects:** Encourage sympathy, relief and misplaced trust.

**Possible objectives:** Influence trust or discourage reporting.

## The Technique

Someone begins recalling or recording a troubling event. Another person responds with apparent shock and disbelief:

> "You sleep deprived him for a whole year?"

If the reaction is deliberately performed, it can make the speaker seem appalled by the alleged behaviour and sympathetic towards the person describing it. The speaker does not need to offer help or explicitly ask for silence. Their apparent concern may be enough to influence how they are perceived.

The key distinction is between **what the speaker expresses** and **what their reaction actually tells you**. An expression of disbelief does not establish the speaker's knowledge, involvement or intentions.

## How It Works

A staged expression of shock could exploit several ordinary social responses:

- **Apparent moral agreement:** The listener seems to share the view that the reported conduct would be unacceptable.
- **Distancing:** Surprise can create the impression that the speaker had no prior knowledge of the event.
- **Emotional reciprocity:** Compassionate behaviour may encourage warmth, trust or sympathy in return.
- **Relief through validation:** Being apparently believed after a distressing experience can feel reassuring.
- **Attention shift:** Instead of continuing to document what happened, the person may start concentrating on whether the listener is an ally.

This can be particularly influential when someone has been isolated, distressed or worried that their account will be dismissed.

## Possible Objective

In a deliberately manipulative interaction, feigned shock might be intended to make the speaker appear sympathetic or uninvolved, reduce the likelihood that the person reports concerns about them, or encourage the person to feel protective towards them.

**The technique would not require anyone to say, "Don't report me."** Any reluctance to report could arise indirectly from the apparent emotional connection.

Another possible effect is to move attention away from the original event and towards the speaker's reaction. Neither effect establishes that this was the speaker's objective in a particular encounter.

## Recognising the Pattern

The timing can be noticeable: a dramatic reaction occurs just as someone begins recalling, documenting or discussing a serious concern.

But genuine shock can look exactly the same. People sometimes react awkwardly, repeat what they have heard in disbelief, or express sincere sympathy. A single remark cannot tell you which explanation is correct.

In particular, if the words were overheard rather than addressed to you, avoid assuming who was being discussed or what the speaker knew. Record the words and context separately from any interpretation.

## Further Considerations

You can accept that a reaction *appeared* compassionate without deciding whether it was sincere. You also do not owe someone trust, loyalty or silence merely because they appeared upset by what you described.

Keep decisions about reporting grounded in the underlying events, available records and appropriate channels, rather than in a third party's display of emotion. There is no need to test the speaker or attempt to expose their motives.

## Response

**Don't engage at all. Record if convenient, then get on with your day.**

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, get to safety and call **999**. Seeking emergency help is not engaging with a provocation.

Read [the Golden Rule chapter](https://mikemayne.github.io/surviving-a-psyop/#read/never_engage).

**If you don't respond, they have nothing to work with.**

**If you don't react, you don't give them anything to work with.**
